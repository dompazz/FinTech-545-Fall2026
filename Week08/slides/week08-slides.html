Risk and Return Attribution

Where Week 07 Left Off

Week 07 built portfolios.

This week takes one apart and asks where its risk and its return actually came from.

That is the question anyone who has to explain a quarter to a client is being asked.

Two exercises, same machinery run in opposite directions. Attribution takes a portfolio and allocates its risk and return to the pieces. Risk budgeting picks the allocation of risk first and solves for the portfolio. Both rest on one fact about homogeneous functions.

Ex-Post and Ex-Ante

Attribution finds the contribution to return or risk from underlying factors.

Ex-post looks at realized risk and return. How much did this stock add to the total return, and to the realized standard deviation?

Ex-ante looks at assumed forward distributions and finds the contributions to expected risk and return.

The Decomposition

Estimate the first derivative and use

f(x) \approx \sum_{i}^{n} x_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}

Each term is one input’s contribution to the total.

Euler’s Theorem

If f is homogeneous of degree 1, f(\lambda x) = \lambda f(x), then

f(x) = \sum_{i}^{n} x_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}

exactly, with no error term.

Both Risk Measures Qualify

Positive homogeneity is the property from Week 05 and Week 07.

Portfolio standard deviation and expected shortfall are both homogeneous of degree 1 in the weights.

The contributions add to the total exactly. Any residual in your code is an arithmetic error, not a modeling one.

Return Attribution

The portfolio return is the sum of the weights times the arithmetic returns:

R_t = \sum_{i}^{n} r_{i,t} w_{i,t}

The derivative with respect to r_i is just the weight. The contribution of factor i at time t is w_i r_i.

Why Arithmetic Returns

Week 04: arithmetic returns are additive across assets inside a period.

That is exactly what an attribution needs.

Linking Through Time

Returns Do Not Add Across Time

The same arithmetic returns are not summable across periods.

The total return is not the sum of the period returns, so the total attribution is not the sum of the period contributions.

We need a way to link them. The most straightforward is Cariño’s K.

Cariño’s K

The arithmetic and geometric total returns:

R = \left[ \prod_{t}^{T} \left( 1 + R_t \right) \right] - 1, \qquad GR = \ln(R + 1)

Their ratio:

K = \frac{GR}{R}

A Scale for Every Period

k_t = \frac{\ln\left( R_t + 1 \right)}{K R_t}

By construction,

R = \sum_{t}^{T} R_t k_t

Check It Once by Hand

Substitute k_t:

\sum_{t}^{T} R_t k_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{t}^{T} \ln\left( 1 + R_t \right)

The sum of logs is the log of the product, and the log of the product is GR.

\frac{GR}{K} = R

Attribution for Each Factor

Inside a period the components are summable:

R = \sum_{t}^{T} k_t \sum_{i}^{n} w_{i,t} r_{i,t}

So the total attribution for factor i is

A_i = \sum_{t}^{T} k_t w_{i,t} r_{i,t}

The Weights Drift

Unless the portfolio is rebalanced every period, the weights move with the market.

Grow each weight by its return:

w_{i,t}^{*} = w_{i,t}\left( 1 + r_{i,t} \right)

Normalize

The portfolio return falls out of the grown weights:

R_t = \left( \sum_{i}^{n} w_{i,t}^{*} \right) - 1

Normalize to get the next period’s starting weight:

w_{i,t+1} = \frac{w_{i,t}^{*}}{R_t + 1}

One pass gives both the portfolio returns and the weights at every period.

Do Not Skip It

A position that doubles while the rest of the book is flat carries a very different weight at the end than at the start.

Attributing its later returns at the original weight misstates every line of the report.

And it does so without any error large enough to notice.

Risk Attribution

Volatility as the Risk

\sigma_p = \sqrt{w'\Sigma w}

The gradient:

\frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w} = \frac{\Sigma w}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}

For an individual w_i, take the i^{th} row.

Component Volatility

CSD = w\, \#\, \frac{\Sigma w}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}

\# is element wise multiplication.

By Euler, the components sum to \sigma_p.

What CSD Is Not

It is not the risk of holding position i on its own.

The components do not add to the sum of the individual volatilities. They add to the portfolio volatility, the smaller number.

The difference is diversification. The gradient allocates it across the positions instead of leaving it as an unexplained residual.

The Gradient Is a Covariance

\frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = \frac{1}{\sigma_p}\sum_{j}^{n}\left( w_j \Sigma_{i,j} \right)

The sum is the definition of \operatorname{cov}(x_i, p), so

RA_i = \frac{w_i \operatorname{cov}(x_i,p)}{\sigma_p} = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(w_i x_i, p)}{\sigma_p}

It Is a Regression

The OLS \beta from Week 02:

\beta = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(y, x)}{\sigma_x^2}

Regress the weighted returns on the portfolio return:

w_i x_i = c + \beta_{i,p}\, p + \epsilon \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad RA_i = \sigma_p \beta_{i,p}

Ex-Post Needs Only Returns

Ex-ante risk attribution needs a covariance matrix.

Ex-post needs only a series of realized returns and a regression.

Week 02 machinery, applied to a question Week 02 never asked.

Beta Is a Share

Divide both sides by \sigma_p:

\frac{RA_i}{\sigma_p} = \beta_{i,p}

The beta of position i against the portfolio is its fraction of total portfolio risk.

A beta of 0.30 is 30% of the volatility, whatever the volatility happens to be.

The Betas Sum to 1

Regressing the portfolio on itself gives a coefficient of 1.

The regression is linear in its dependent variable, so the individual betas add to that.

Shares that sum to 1 are shares. It is also a free check on the arithmetic.

The Setup

Construct the maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio for AAPL, MSFT, BRK-B, CSCO, and JNJ.

The expected returns come from a factor model, not from sample means.

Expected Returns From Factors

  1. Join the Fama French 3 factor file to momentum, divide by 100, join to DailyReturn.csv
  2. Regress each stock on [\text{Mkt-RF},\ \text{SMB},\ \text{HML},\ \text{Mom}] with an intercept. Keep the betas, discard the intercept
  3. Each factor’s expected daily return is its mean over the last 10 years
  4. Geometric, annualized at 255 days:

\mu_i = 255 \cdot \ln\left( 1 + \sum_{j} \beta_{i,j} E(f_j) \right)

Covariance From the Stocks

\Sigma = 255 \cdot \operatorname{cov}\left( \ln(1 + Y) \right)

Maximize the Sharpe Ratio subject to \sum w_i = 1 and w_i \geq 0, at a risk free rate of 25bp.

A non-linear objective. It needs a solver, not a closed form.

Why Not a Factor Covariance

We are assuming multivariate normality. A multivariate normal is completely described by \mu and \Sigma.

Splitting \Sigma into a factor covariance and idiosyncratic variances would only add estimation error to a quantity we can already get in one step.

T \gg n

Five stocks against several years of daily data. \Sigma is full rank and comfortably estimated.

Every Week 03 concern about singular covariance matrices is absent.

Check that before reusing this setup. As the number of assets approaches the length of the series, the sample covariance degrades and then fails. That is Week 09.

Attribution to a Factor

From Positions to Factors

So far, risk and return went to the positions.

Describe each asset with m factors instead:

x_i = \alpha + \beta_i F + \epsilon, \qquad \beta_i F = \sum_{j}^{m} \beta_{i,j} F_j

The Residual

Unless \alpha = 0 and \sigma_e = 0, the factors will not explain everything.

Collect the error:

e_i = \alpha_i + \epsilon_i

p = \sum_{i}^{n} w_i \sum_{j}^{m} \beta_{i,j} F_j + \sum_{i}^{n} w_i e_i

Portfolio Alpha

Attribute the error to each asset, or sum it and treat it as one more factor.

In ex-post analysis that total is the portfolio alpha:

\alpha_p = \sum_{i}^{n} w_i e_i

Two Names, One Residual

Return side. Portfolio alpha. The part of the return the factors did not explain, which is what an active manager is paid for.

Risk side. Idiosyncratic, specific, or residual risk. The part of the volatility that did not come from factor exposure.

Plenty of reports use one term in the return table and the other in the risk table without saying so. Ask which is meant.

Collapsing to Factor Weights

Define a factor weight:

w_j = \sum_{i}^{n} w_i \beta_{i,j}

p = \sum_{j}^{m} w_j F_j + \alpha_p

The Whole Trick

A portfolio of n assets has become a portfolio of m factors plus a residual.

If m < n, the problem got smaller.

Nothing after this point knows or cares that the factors are not assets.

The Recipe

  1. Calculate factor weights through time
  2. Calculate portfolio return through time
  3. Calculate alpha through time
  4. Calculate the Cariño K scaling factors
  5. Perform the return attribution
  6. Regress on the portfolio for the risk attribution

To push the residual back to the assets, expand \alpha_p = \sum w_i e_i. The math is the same.

Risk Budgeting and Risk Parity

Is Optimized Optimal?

Richard Michaud, 1989, “The Markowitz Optimization Enigma: Is ‘Optimized’ Optimal?”

Small changes to correlations, volatilities, and expected values change the portfolio dramatically.

“Mean Variance Optimization is Error Maximizing.”

Or as Yogi Berra Said

“It’s tough to make predictions, especially about the future.”

The Mechanism Is Week 01

The standard error of an estimated mean is \sigma/\sqrt{n}.

For returns, \sigma dwarfs \mu.

The optimizer takes the noisy means at face value and loads up on whichever asset happened to look best.

What We Know Better

Long term plans use estimated returns and covariance. In the long run, risk premia are fairly stable.

Shorter term, we are less confident about expected return.

We are more confident about volatilities, and to some extent correlations.

Risk Budgets

Ex-ante risk contributions as a percentage of total risk show how much risk each position is given.

Without a good view on expected return, control how much risk each position uses instead.

That is risk budgeting.

Risk Parity

Set the risk budgets equal.

CSD = w\, \#\, \frac{\Sigma w}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}, \qquad SSE = \sum_{i}^{n} \left( CSD_i - \overline{CSD} \right)^2

\min SSE \qquad s.t.\ \sum_{i}^{n} w_i = 1

Least Squares, Non-Linear

It is a least squares problem.

The square root and the inverse of portfolio volatility make it non-linear.

It needs a non-linear optimizer.

No Expected Return Anywhere

No expected return appears in the optimization.

No estimated mean can distort the answer.

That is the entire appeal.

Inverse Volatility

w_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} \cdot \frac{1}{\sum_{j}^{n} \frac{1}{\sigma_j}}

An approximation to risk parity.

Good starting values if the optimizer has trouble converging.

A Chain of Conditions

All correlations equal: risk parity is inverse volatility.

All correlations equal and all Sharpe Ratios equal: risk parity is also the maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio.

Long term Sharpe Ratios roughly equal: inverse volatility approximates the super efficient portfolio, with no means or correlations estimated.

Why It Is Defensible

Not a claim that equal risk shares are inherently right.

Under two assumptions we are more willing to make than a forecast of means, equal risk shares land on the portfolio the Week 07 optimization would have chosen.

Risk Parity with Unequal Risk Budgets

Expressing a View

Portfolio managers often have an opinion about which assets will outperform.

Give high conviction positions a larger risk budget and low conviction ones a smaller one.

b = \left[ b_1, b_2,\ \dots,\ b_n \right]

Twice the budget of everything else: b_2 = 2 b_i \ \ \forall\ i \neq 2.

Scale the Components

CSD^{*} = \left[ \frac{CSD_1}{b_1}, \frac{CSD_2}{b_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{CSD_n}{b_n} \right]

SSE = \sum_{i}^{n} \left( CSD_i^{*} - \overline{CSD^{*}} \right)^2, \qquad \min SSE \quad s.t.\ \sum_{i}^{n} w_i = 1

Strictly More General

Set every b_i equal and the division changes nothing.

Plain risk parity is the special case where the manager has no view.

Non-Normal Risk Parity

Beyond Volatility

Every risk parity portfolio so far assumed multivariate normality and used volatility.

Returns are not normal. We need a more robust risk measure.

VaR Will Not Do

VaR is not coherent.

One implication: the VaR surface is non-convex. Optimizations may not converge, and can converge to a local optimum that is not the global one.

ES is coherent, and convex.

Component Expected Shortfall

ES(w) = \sum_{i}^{n} w_i \frac{\partial ES}{\partial w_i}

Euler again. ES is positively homogeneous, property 3 of coherence in Week 05.

The decomposition is exact for the same reason it was exact for volatility.

No Closed Form

Without a known distribution, ES has no closed form gradient.

Estimate it with a finite difference on the simulated returns R:

\frac{\partial ES}{\partial w_i} \approx \frac{ES\left( \left[ w_1,\ \dots,\ w_i + e,\ \dots,\ w_n \right]\ |\ R \right) - ES(w\ |\ R)}{e}

The forward difference from Week 07.

Hold R Fixed

Use the same simulated return matrix for both evaluations.

The numerator is a difference of two nearly equal numbers.

Redraw R between them and the simulation noise dominates the derivative.

The Same Optimization

CES = w\, \#\, \frac{\partial ES}{\partial w}, \qquad SSE = \sum_{i}^{n} \left( CES_i - \overline{CES} \right)^2

\min SSE \qquad s.t.\ \sum_{i}^{n} w_i = 1

Only the component risk changed. Divide by b for unequal budgets, as before.
